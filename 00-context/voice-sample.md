# Voice Sample

Used by: [[uc-writer]], [[uc-humanizer]], [[committee-review]]

## Voice notes (what to copy)
- Opens inside a concrete scene, often with a person doing something or a blunt fact ("Three runners were injured in two weeks.").
- Short blunt sentences mixed with longer reflective ones. Fragments allowed ("Silence. Then: ...").
- Real dialogue in straight quotes, short and plain.
- Admits doubt and failure plainly ("I wanted to quit.", "I drafted the message three times and deleted it.").
- Specific numbers everywhere: weeks, counts, times, minutes.
- Plain vocabulary; first person; past tense; few adjectives.
- Shows a setback mid-story, then a turn, then an earned one-to-two sentence takeaway at the end.
- Light punctuation: commas, periods, a colon now and then; almost no dashes.

--- SAMPLE 1 (VOICE ONLY) ---
Arjun sat with his arms crossed, staring at the grass during our first meditation session at the park. When I asked him to try box-breathing, he muttered, "My parents made me come." I recognized that resistance. Freshman year, I'd been the same way when suffering from panic attacks until fifteen-minutes of meditation brought me control again. I collaborated with my heartfulness meditation instructor to launch weekly sessions as an independent initiative. By week six, Arjun was arriving early to help set up mats.

Weeks later, I saw Arjun teaching box-breathing to a kid before a session started. He emailed me asking if his brother could also attend: "I never thought I'd be the one helping someone. The breathing actually worked!" But I'd found that most students only practiced during our sessions. Fifteen-minute videos that I designed were not helping, and kids mentioned it was hard to focus for that long when anxious.

I spent two months building BrighterMinds, a website with three-minute videos organized into Spotify-style playlists: "Before a Test," "Can't Sleep," "Angry?" Practice rates skyrocketed. A student who'd ignored my emails told me she'd watched the "Can't Sleep" video four times before her final. But every session depended on me showing up. I'd been so focused on teaching meditation correctly that I'd made myself indispensable.

I stopped trying to reach every student myself and started training three peer instructors who introduced eighteen new students and brought their own approaches. One added yoga for restless students and another introduced sessions with music. When Arjun sent a picture of his first time teaching his middle school session with nine middle-schoolers in a circle practicing box-breathing, I barely recognized my own program. They weren't just using my techniques, they'd adapted and refined them.

The success of the program wasn't because I held onto control, but because I'd let go. Now, with thirty students in my sessions and peer instructors hosting sessions separately, the work continues whether I'm there or not. That's exactly how it should be. Through this I learned that true community service is creating a space for others to lead.

--- SAMPLE 2 (VOICE ONLY) ---
Three runners were injured in two weeks. Our new coach was running us into the ground, and as captain, I'd said nothing.

When our distance coach left after my sophomore year, he'd been replaced by the sprints coach. Under sprint-style training, injuries spiked, times plateaued, and freshmen went all-out every workout pressuring everyone into unstable paces.

Mile 7 of our Sunday long run, my teammate Nikhil said what we'd all been thinking: "I miss Coach Esayas. I haven't improved in months and I'm one workout from injury." I realized my silence would only guarantee more suffering.

I drafted the message to coach three times and deleted it. Who was I to question his training? I was just a high schooler. He'd coached for 15 years. Watching another teammate get injured on the track, I hit send before I can second-guess again.

"Are you saying I'm incompetent?" Coach asked skeptically. I retracted, "No..., what if I manage the paces with your workouts? Based on 5K times, I'd calculate tempo paces so runners improve safely." Silence. Then: "One-month trial, if nothing improves, it's back to normal."

I arrived early to practices, calculated paces and paired runners with similar race times to prevent racing during workouts. Between reps, I'd pull people aside: "That's too fast, dial back five seconds." Half the team ignored my pace calls by week two, complaining about my slow times in the group chat, and kept racing workouts. I wanted to quit. But Nikhil texted me: "My shin-splints are healing for the first time in months. What's next?"

I stayed late helping underclassmen and gradually, more people listened to my advice. After placing last in the league the year before, fall 2023 was the best season in our school's history, we finished one spot away from State Championships. After our last meet, Nikhil found me: "Broke 5:00 in the mile today. I owe it to you". Leadership meant acting between what authority designs and what people need. Most difficult part wasn't developing a system, it was acting before I felt ready, hoping trying would beat silence.
